# Summary

A club-good model of religious and other voluntary groups. Each member's utility depends on
private goods, their own participation and the group's "quality", which rises with what the other
members put in, so the good shows positive returns to "participatory crowding" (271). Because
participation cannot be monitored, the group suffers a free-rider problem, and seemingly
unproductive costs can mitigate it: they screen out people whose participation would be low while
raising participation among those who join (272). The formal setup takes group quality as
Q = F(R̄, N), a function of the other members' average participation and the group's size, and
assumes F strictly concave in both arguments and strictly increasing in size (277). Prohibitions
on close substitutes for participation can raise members' utility (proposition 1), shown in a
numerical example with Cobb-Douglas club production in which quality is simply the others' average
participation, Q = R̄ (280). One-time costs such as painful initiations are explained as screening
devices in a heterogeneous population (281).

## What it supports here, and what it does not

- **Chapter 2's use holds.** Costly requirements that exclude free-riders and strengthen those who
  remain is the paper's mechanism (272, 281). Applying it to the Sons of Temperance's screening at
  the door is the chapter's reading, not the paper's; the paper never mentions temperance.
- **Chapters 12 and 15 claimed too much.** Both credited the paper with "the saturation form" of
  the participatory resources. What the paper supplies is the premise, that group quality rises
  with members' participation and with group size at a diminishing rate (277). The form the model
  uses, capacity = c / (c + k) in the number of engaged members, which also caps each resource, is
  this book's choice. The paper's own worked example is linear in participation (280). Both
  chapters were corrected on 29 September 2026.
