# Summary

Latent class growth analysis of how often 586 alcohol-dependent people went to AA meetings over
seven years. The sample combines a treatment cohort recruited from the ten qualifying programmes in
one northern California county with a general-population cohort recruited by random-digit dialling
and untreated in the prior year; telephone follow-ups at 1, 3, 5 and 7 years. Over the period, 75
per cent attended at least one AA meeting and 85 per cent entered a treatment programme at least
once.

Four attendance classes fitted best: low, 63 per cent, n = 371, fewer than five meetings at most
follow-ups; medium, 16 per cent, n = 90, about 50 meetings a year at each follow-up; descending,
11 per cent, n = 67, about 150 meetings in year one, then a steep decline that then stabilises;
and high, 10 per cent, n = 58, about 200 meetings at year one declining steadily to year seven.
The growth parameters are in the paper's Table 2. Attendance rose from baseline to year one in all
four classes and fell thereafter except in the medium class, which was flat.

The finding the authors emphasise is that declines in attendance were not matched by declines in
abstinence: the descending class's abstinence stayed high, about three quarters reporting 30-day
abstinence at year seven, as did the high class's. At year seven, 46 per cent of the high class had
a sponsor, 74 per cent had helped newcomers and 80 per cent considered themselves members; for the
descending class the figures were 35, 44 and 74 per cent. Treatment exposure was minimally related
to attendance in all classes except the descending one, where the relationship was negative once
the time trend was accounted for, which the authors read as substitution between treatment and
meetings.

**Read in full on 22 September 2026**, apart from the three figures, which are images and are not
in the deposited author manuscript.

What it supports here is the slow end of the bracket around the model's decay rate, and only that.
It measures attendance, not practice, and attendance is what the model treats as an input to
practice rather than practice itself. What it establishes is that participation in the book's own
fellowship falls away on a scale of years rather than weeks, and that a group's members can stop
attending without the outcome following, which is a caution against reading the model's
practice variable as a proxy for meeting counts. Used in Chapter 12 and the paper's limitations.
