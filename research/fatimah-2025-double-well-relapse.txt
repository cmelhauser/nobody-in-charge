Abstract

Author Manuscript

Substance use relapse is difficult to define, and previous work has used one-size-fits-all ad-hoc
definitions. Researchers have called for a dynamic and personalized understanding of relapse as a
concept and model, necessitating novel statistical tools. We aimed to develop and validate a novel
statistical model of latent relapse processes: the double-well potential model (DWPM). This model
describes post-treatment substance use in terms of a dynamical system with stable equilibria
of abstinence and relapse, a person-specific dominant equilibria (tilt), the ease of changing
between equilibria (steepness), and an overall relapse risk (RR). Using timeline followback data
from N=139 adults with a substance use disorder transitioning back to the community after
residential treatment, we examined individual differences and the criterion-related validity of
DWPM parameters to determine the clinical utility of the double-well model. While non-use
was the predominant stable state across participants, we found significant between-subjects
variability steepness and RR. These individual differences were predictable via demographics,
baseline psychopathology, treatment history, and treatment condition. Steepness and RR also
predicted long-term outcomes, including life satisfaction and criminal behavior, above and beyond
traditional metrics of relapse (proportion of days used, time to first use). Thus, the DWPM is a
strong theoretical and statistical representation of the underlying relapse processes. Moreover, the
parameters show criterion-related validity and may be useful in precision medicine.

Researchers have called for a dynamic and personalized understanding of relapse as a concept
and model. We offer a non-linear dynamical system – a double-well potential model that defines
relapse in terms of two equilibria (an abstinence well and use well) of a continuous dynamical

Author Manuscript

INTRODUCTION

Author Manuscript

A long-standing problem in addiction research is the conceptual and empirical definition of
relapse following treatment or a period of abstinence (Miller et al., 2001; Witkiewitz, 2005;
Witkiewitz & Marlatt, 2004; Maisto et al., 2003; McKay et al., 2006). Recent systematic
reviews identified up to 101 different definitions of alcohol use disorder relapse (Maisto
et al., 2016; Sliedrecht et al., 2022), including but not limited to any drinking, any heavy
drinking, consumption of 2+ or 4+ standard drinks, consumption of 6+ standard drinks
on a single day, consumption on 3+ or 7+ consecutive days, return to treatment, and
consequences of consumption. Likewise, Maisto identified seven different definitions for
non-alcohol substance relapse including any use, more than two episodes of use, return
to previous patterns of use and admission to treatment. This heterogeneity is unsurprising,
because post-abstinence patterns of use vary greatly within and between individuals, ranging
from infrequent, moderate use to daily, heavy use; discontinuities and non-linearity in
trajectories are quite common as well (Chow et al., 2014; Maisto et al., 2020; Witkiewitz
et al., 2010, 2017; Witkiewitz & Masyn, 2008). There is clearly a need to move away
from post-hoc, one-size-fits-all definitions of relapse to a more nuanced understanding of
the construct. Here, we propose to define relapse in terms of the equilibria of a dynamical
system. In such a system, we define two stable locations, or equilibria, in continuous
behavior space: one for abstinence and one for relapse. When a person’s latent position is
in the vicinity of the abstinence equilibrium location, the person is likely to show no use,
or use that does not rise to the level of relapse; when the latent position is in the vicinity of
the relapse location, a pattern of problem use is likely. Although this latent position varies
continuously, it is always in the vicinity of one of these two equilibrium locations.

Author Manuscript

In part, the definition of relapse is a theoretical issue. Maisto and colleagues (Maisto et al.,
2016) have stated that the definition of relapse should be linked to the theoretical model
of the phenotype in question. For instance, adopting the 12-Step model of addiction (i.e.,
“one drink, one drunk”) suggests a framework in which even a single instance of use defines
relapse. In contrast, models such as the Transtheoretical Model of change (Prochaska, 2020)
and the cognitive-behavioral relapse prevention model (Marlatt, 1996; Witkiewitz & Marlatt,
2004, 2007), draw a distinction between lapse and relapse, where lapses are occasional
instants of use that do not necessarily lead to a full-blown relapse, but are correctable,

temporary setbacks. These models propose that relapse is part of a fluid and dynamic
process of change. Although the details of these models differ, in general, both postulate
that a single instance of use does not imply resistance to change, that change is frequently
a function of many attempts, and that a patient may fluctuate in and out of ‘relapse’ after
treatment – perhaps many times before achieving a desired level of abstinence or low
risk use. A model of relapse should also have construct (in particular, criterion-related)
validity (Maisto et al., 2016). Stated otherwise, a given definition of relapse should be
meaningfully predicted by known addiction-related processes (e.g., negative emotionality,
psychopathology, addiction severity, abstinence motivation), and meaningfully predict other,
downstream clinical outcomes (e.g., legal problems, independent living, employment,
psychopathology, life satisfaction). When these conditions are met, the model of relapse
can be used for theory testing as well as prevention, triage, or treatment planning.

Yet, in many ways, the definition of relapse is a practical issue, limited by commonly used
statistical tools that often fail to map on to the verbal theory. Very common are survival
analyses predicting time to first use (Harvey et al., 2016; Kassani et al., 2015). These
assume, implicitly, that a single use is equivalent to relapse, but do not consider patients’
subsequent behavior after the first instance of use. Some models employ strict definitions
of number of uses overall or per week, implicitly suggesting that once these clinical cutoffs
are passed, the patient is at risk for severe long-term outcomes (e.g., (De Sousa, 2014).
Other methods include negative binomial hurdle or zero-inflated Poisson regression models
(Amirabadizadeh et al., 2021; Bowen et al., 2014), which allow one to detect and predict
both a binary use/non-use status, as well as continuous proportion of days used. Yet, these
models do not handle heterogeneity in the patterns or dynamics of use (i.e., when in the time
series substance use occurred and whether a significant period(s) of abstinence appeared in
the series). Nor do these models allow one to conceptualize relapse as a dynamic process
where (1) both continual use and steady non-use are self-perpetuating behaviors, but also
(2) exogenous disturbances can either lead to use or non-use. Hence, the statistical models
applied in relapse literature are not aligned with theoretical understanding of relapse as a
process that includes both use and non-use behaviors that change over time. Unsurprisingly,
addiction researchers have called for the development of quantitative models that handle the
conceptual and empirical complexity describing return to substance use following abstinence
or treatment (Chow et al., 2014; Hufford et al., 2003; Maisto et al., 2014; Resnicow &
Page, 2008; Witkiewitz & Marlatt, 2004, 2007). Particularly, Maisto and colleagues (Maisto
et al., 2016) highlighted the need to describe relapse as a process and distinguish it from
isolated instances of use. Moreover, Maisto called for utilizing dynamic, person-centered
data and models to describe lapse and relapse as a part of a change process rather than as an
endpoint. This is especially needed given the increasing number of studies utilizing real or
near-real-time data collection and intensive longitudinal designs in studying post-abstinence
patterns of use. To date, models that were developed to handle intraindividual complexity
and nonlinear dynamics (e.g., cusp catastrophe, regime switching, damped oscillator) have
been shown to be superior to traditional models in terms of fit and variance explained
(Hufford et al., 2003; Witkiewitz et al., 2010; Witkiewitz & Marlatt, 2007). They are also
flexible enough to predict sudden, nonlinear shifts between periods of use (‘relapse’) and
relative non-use (‘abstinence’). Finally, many such models can be fit to a single patient, and

Author Manuscript

Previously, we developed a novel dynamical systems model to capture and forecast the
dynamics of relapse (Hunter et al., 2022). Consistent with many current theories of relapse
(Chow et al., 2014; Witkiewitz & Marlatt, 2007), the double-well potential model (DWPM)
postulates that both ‘relapse’ and ‘abstinence’ can be modeled as stable attractors in a
continuous latent space, also conceptualized as two ‘wells’ between which a patient can
traverse. Underlying binary use and non-use data, there may be a continuous latent process
that can shift the system from non-use to use and vice versa. Critically, the latent process
varies smoothly over time along a continuum, but this latent process is attracted to two
points: one that represents ‘relapse’ and one that represents ‘abstinence’. One could imagine
a steel ball rolling on a table underneath which are two magnets of different strengths; the
ball rolls smoothly but is attracted by the magnets. The DWPM uses the analogy of potential
energy to describe the separation between these two stable wells. When the separation
energy is large, a greater disturbance is required to shift the system from one well to the
other. Based on the difference between the amount of disturbance required to push the
system from non-use to use versus from use to non-use, we can compute personalized risk of
relapse.

Author Manuscript

The DWPM provides at least two parameters of interest: the overall stability or steepness,
indicating the absolute ease or difficulty in traversing between the stable wells, and the
relative stability of the wells or tilt detecting whether one of them is more stable and difficult
to leave than the other. It also provides an individualized relapse risk (RR), which is a
function of both the steepness and tilt parameters and represents the difference in separation
energies from each well to the other. Initially, we developed a nomothetic model, such that
the same form and parameter values applied to all individuals in the study. However, there
are likely predictable individual differences around both the steepness and tilt parameters.
We examined the association of the interindividual differences in these parameters with
exogenous variables. The goal is to allow quantitative differences between people while
maintaining the same basic process for all people, trying to balance competing goals of
person-specific processes and generalizable laws of human behavior (Hunter et al., 2024). If
individual differences are longitudinally related to addiction-related clinical predictors and
outcomes, then the DWPM of relapse may be both theoretically and clinically useful.

Author Manuscript

The goals of the current study were twofold: 1) to extend the DWPM to encompass
individual differences in the steepness and tilt parameters, and 2) to examine the
criterion-related validity of this relapse model by examining the prospective relationships
of model parameters with clinical predictors and outcomes. In doing so, we utilized
a longitudinal dataset of individuals with substance use disorders who were seeking
residential addiction treatment and were followed into the community for ~six months after
treatment completion. Given that all patients were enrolled in a randomized clinical trial
(NCT01741415; Bornovalova et al., n.d.; Choate et al., 2021), we examined the impact of

METHOD
Participants

Author Manuscript

We used data from 139 patients with SUD participating in a larger randomized clinical
trial. Between April 2013 and October 2017, 374 patients at a residential treatment for
SUD were recruited to participate in a randomized clinical trial with two conditions: Skills
for Improving Distress Intolerance (SIDI) and Supportive Counseling (SC). Comparison of
these two conditions on the primary outcomes of post-treatment and follow-up changes in
distress tolerance and substance use is the focus of another report (Bornovalova et al, under
review). The treatment facility serves as a jail diversion program for substance users but
accepts voluntary admissions as well. Treatment involves strategies adopted from 12-step
programs and group sessions focused on psychoeducation and relapse prevention. Before
entry, participants are detoxified as needed, and complete abstinence (except nicotine) is
required during the stay. Minimum stay is 30 days; beyond, participants are encouraged to
stay until reaching psychosocial stabilization. To be eligible for the parent study, participants
had to give informed consent, be at least 18 years old, evidence DSM-IV substance
dependence, receive at least one treatment session in the randomized clinical trial, and
plan to reside within the 6-county (within ≈ 2.5-hour drive) catchment area of the residential
treatment site for at least six months after completion of treatment.

Of the initial sample (374), 354 participants were eligible for the parent study, and only

Analyses
We modelled substance use behavior as a continuous dynamical system with two stable
latent states of abstinence and relapse reflecting observed non-use and use, respectively. To
this aim, we fit the double-well potential model (DWPM) to the TLFB data. The DWPM is a
non-linear, continuous time model described by the following equation:

The DWPM represents the rate of change of the continuous latent use propensity, dx/dt, as
a non-linear function of the current latent use propensity, x, a dynamic noise component,
q(t), and estimated parameters a and b. The DWPM has three fixed points or equilibria (i.e.,
values of x such that dx/dt=0): use (x=1) and non-use (x=0) are the two stable locations in
behavior space, or attractors represented by ‘wells’ in Figure 1; b (henceforth called tilt)
is the unstable position or repellor represented by the hill between the two wells. Because
locations of the two stable wells are fixed, the location of the peak between them represents
how favored one well is over the other. An analogy is a ball rolling on top of this hill. The
higher the hill-top between the two wells, the more difficult it would be for the ball to move
from one well to the other (abstinence to relapse or the reverse). This difficulty in changing
between the stable use and non-use locations in behavior space is represented by the a
parameter (henceforth called steepness), with higher values indicating more difficulty and
therefore higher overall stability. Note that stability is a double-edged sword: it perpetuates
the current behavior regardless of it being use or non-use. The tilt parameter indicates the
relative stability of the wells. It is represented by the position of the hill between the two
wells: if the hill is closer to 0 or the non-use well, then the use well will be deeper, making
it is easier to change from non-use to use. Likewise, if the hill is closer to 1 or the use well,
then the non-use well will be deeper and more attractive to the ball. Figure 1a-b displays
how the shape of the potential well will vary under different steepness and tilt parameter
values. Of note, the DWPM estimates a continuously varying latent use propensity that
inhabits the non-use and use wells, which is consistent with many current theories. The
latent use propensity varies both qualitatively – inhabiting one of the two wells – and
quantitatively – moving within one well and approaching the other.
We fit the DWPM using the package dynr (Ou et al., 2019) in R 3.3.3 with steepness and
tilt parameters as random effects. That is, the steepness and tilt parameters were allowed
to vary across individuals but were assumed to stay constant over time within individuals.

As step 2, we added a set of baseline variables as covariates to the DWPM including
demographics, psychopathology, treatment history etc., Person-specific estimates for the
steepness and tilt parameters were extracted from the random effects model and saved for
subsequent analyses. Based on the a and b parameters, we calculated a person-specific
risk of relapse (RR) as the difference in the depths of the two wells with higher values
indicating greater risk using the following equation: RR = a - 2ab/12. This RR value is a
metric of the difference in the separation energies of the two wells. Finally, we assessed the
associations between the person-specific a and b parameters and clinical outcomes including
symptom severity, life satisfaction, criminal behaviors and incarceration, employment, and
government dependence using Pearson and polyserial correlations. As this is an initial
exploration of the utility of the DWPM, we primarily focus on the effect sizes in our
interpretation.

RESULTS
As a first step, we fit a DWPM with random effects, allowing both steepness and tilt
parameters to vary across individuals.
The tilt parameter value of 0.62 indicated that overall the non-use well was deeper or
more stable than the use well. Results showed statistically significant variance across
individuals in the steepness, but not in the tilt parameter (see Table 2). Tilt was dropped as a
random effect in subsequent analyses due to lack of variance across individuals. Therefore,
associations with baseline and follow-up correlates were examined for steepness only.

Author Manuscript

Figure 2 illustrates the distinction the DWPM makes between lapse and relapse in four
individuals in our sample. The latent use propensity shown in a solid black line defines the
state of an individual at any given time. If the latent state is in the use well, the individual
has relapsed. If the latent state is in the non-use well, the individual is in recovery. Tilt is the
border between the two wells (cf. Figure 1). The DWPM distinguished between the observed
use/non-use of an individual from their latent location. Thus, although a person may have
used a substance (observed state), he/she might not have fully relapsed (latent state). The
latent state is akin to a factor score (Priestly & Subba Rao, 1975), and thus depends on the
nomothetic and person-specific model parameters, along with the individual’s use/non-use
history.

Author Manuscript

The latent state is not a simple, static rule that applies uniformly across all individuals.
However, in certain simple situations, the latent state is equivalent to such a rule (e.g.,
number of days with consecutive use). In simple situations like those of person 348 and
115 in Figure 2, lapse and relapse could easily be defined in terms of days of consecutive
use, and the latent state definition implied by the DWPM would agree with that definition.
Even in these cases, however, the simple rule would depend on the person-specific midpoint
parameter that defines the border between the two wells. Although the tilt parameter did not
differ across people in our sample, if this parameter were closer to zero for some individual,
then a smaller number of consecutive days of use would lead to relapse than a person with a
higher tilt parameter.

In more complicated patterns of use behavior like those of person 251 and 291 in Figure
2, simple definitions of lapse/relapse become inappropriate. The lapses of person 251 are
separated by enough time that the latent state estimate fully returns to the non-use well,
whereas those of person 291 (around time 50) appear to be a building pattern leading to a
short relapse. Because of the recent lapses of person 291, a smaller number of consecutive
days of use are required for the latent state to cross into relapse than without those recent
lapses. The definition of relapse in the DWPM is thus context dependent both on the person
and on their recent use history.
A benefit of the DWPM specifically and the dynamic approach generally is that its definition
of lapse/relapse encompasses both the simple situation and the complicated situation under
the same umbrella without the need for ad hoc exceptions. This model abstracts and
generalizes the notion of lapse/relapse.

As a next step to assess clinical validity of the dynamical parameters, we added baseline
variables as predictors of steepness in the DWPM. The package dynr uses FIML to handle
missing values in the primary variable but does not allow missing values on the predictors,
resulting in the removal of 5 participants (N = 134). In the first block, we included age, sex,
education, whether treatment was court-mandated, and treatment condition (SIDI V. SC) as
predictors. Female gender and SIDI treatment condition significantly, positively predicted
the steepness parameter (Table 3). This block was retained in all subsequent models.

Author Manuscript

In block 2, we added baseline psychopathology variables, inpatient and outpatient treatment
history, and motivation for treatment. The clinician-ascertained DSM-IV diagnoses versus
dimensionally measured PID-5 pathological personality traits and substance use frequencies
were added as two alternative sets to avoid theoretical and empirical redundancy. In set 1
(clinician-ascertained diagnoses), panic disorder, PTSD, cocaine use disorder and inpatient
treatment history positively predicted higher stability, whereas alcohol use disorder showed
the reverse.
Alternatively, in set 2 (dimensional measures of pathological personality traits and substance
use frequencies), detachment, marijuana use frequency, and inpatient treatment history
predicted a higher overall stability, whereas alcohol use frequency predicted lower stability.
Across sets, individuals with higher education and those assigned to SIDI had higher overall
stability.

Finally, we investigated the convergent, discriminant, and criterion-related validity of
steepness and RR. We examined the associations of steepness and RR with typical
indices used (proportion of days used, PDU, and time to first use, TFU). Subsequently,
we examined associations with clinical outcomes including life satisfaction, psychiatric
distress, employment status, independent living, government dependence, maximum number
of reported criminal behaviors, and number of days incarcerated. We used data from the last
available assessment for each participant for the dependent variables. Third, we examined
the incremental utility of RR and steepness predicting outcomes significant in bivariate
analyses over PDU and TFU, as well as vice versa, in a series of hierarchical linear

When examining incremental utility (Table 5), steepness accounted for an additional 2–3%
of the variance in life satisfaction, but the reverse was not true (PDU and FDU accounted
for .2% and <.01% of variance in life satisfaction). Similarly, steepness and RR accounted

over RR. This pattern of results suggests that steepness and RR have unique variance that is
non-redundant with traditional indices of relapse, and this unique variance is meaningful in
predicting outcomes.

DISCUSSION

Author Manuscript

Current ad-hoc definitions of relapse suffer from an insufficient link to theoretical
underpinnings, to the between-person differences in what constitutes relapse, and to the
within-person dynamics of use. In our sample of individuals transitioning to community
after residential addiction treatment, we explored utility and criterion-related validity of the
DWPM – a dynamical systems model – to understand the latent construct of relapse. The
DWPM supposes that relapse and abstinence are two stable locations in a latent, continuous
behavior space between which an individual can move, while accounting for individual
differences in this process. Importantly, this model conceptually matches many current
theories and empirical studies (Maisto et al., 2020; Witkiewitz & Marlatt, 2009), in that it
estimates membership in use versus non-use wells on a continuous scale. It also allows a
personalized definition of relapse, including the estimation of a personalized risk of relapse
(RR) based on the personalized dynamic model parameters.

Author Manuscript

In our sample, we found abstinence to be a more stable state than relapse, with the individual
differences in this parameter (tilt) being non-significant. However, individuals differed
significantly in steepness, or the overall stability or the ease/difficulty in traversing between
the two wells, once again underscoring the utility of personalized approach to definitions of
relapse. Therefore, the model accounts for varying implications of a single instance of use
for different individuals. For instance, the model suggests that if an individual had a more
stable abstinence state (tilt > 0.5) and a high overall stability (larger value of steepness), a
single lapse may not tip the system into relapse. In contrast, a single drink or instance of use
may have a much larger impact to an individual with a lower overall steepness, as it is easier
for them to traverse between the stable states.

Additionally, we were able to predict these dynamic properties using distal risk factors.
Individuals with higher education levels, baseline diagnoses of PTSD, panic disorder,
cocaine use disorder, greater marijuana use frequency, PID-5 detachment, and a history of
inpatient treatment were more likely to have higher steepness or overall stability. In contrast,
baseline alcohol use disorder and frequency predicted lower steepness or an overall ease
of changing between states. Generally, a high steepness is not inherently indicative of high
or low risk of relapse as that depends on which stable state the individual is in. However,
in our sample the abstinence well was deeper for most participants and steepness was
highly correlated with a lower risk of relapse. Therefore, the findings that certain baseline
diagnoses predicted steepness are in contrast with the large body of research that documents
a positive association between psychopathology and previous substance use on the risk of
SUD relapse (Andersson et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2020). However, a few studies do
show that internalizing symptoms and polysubstance use may serve as a protective factor in
substance use and relapse (Colder et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2021).

Author Manuscript

The current study demonstrates clinical utility of the DWPM as its parameters, and the
resulting personalized risk of relapse (RR), may help predict future clinical outcomes. Our
findings show that steepness and lower RR were prospectively associated with higher life
satisfaction and lower criminal behaviors, important markers of substance use outcomes (Di
Maggio et al., 2022; Laudet et al., 2009; Laudet & Stanick, 2010). Moreover, steepness
and RR were non-redundant with typical indices of relapse (proportion of days used, time
to first use), and incrementally predicted life satisfaction and criminal behaviors above and
beyond these typical indices. Thus, as recommended in development of relapse models,
we demonstrate criterion validity and clinical utility of the DWPM at the between-person
level in the current sample. An additional clinical implication is the ability to differentiate
lapse and relapse, as the nature of the clinical intervention differs between the two (Abed
& Ansari Shahidi, 2019). Post-lapse, a clinician would perhaps focus on ‘lessons learned’;
post-relapse, a more intensive level of care may be warranted.
Relatedly, it is worth noting that we accounted for treatment condition in all models, and
higher stability was predicted by SIDI. This suggests that SIDI may have potential not
just to reduce overall levels of use but the latent underlying process. We are hesitant to
overinterpret this finding, as a) the current study was not intended to be a treatment outcome
analysis, and b) our analyses were focused on individuals with variance in community
substance use. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that the effect of condition is theoretically
and empirically consistent (Bowen et al., 2014; Ellis et al., 2022) and robust to covariates,
suggesting a promising avenue for future studies. On a broader note, the DWPM parameters
may be interesting outcomes in clinical trials.

Author Manuscript

Seven limitations should be considered. First, we used a range of established predictors
of relapse such as baseline substance use, personality, and psychopathology. However,
these did not cover the full relapse matrix of distal variables proposed by modern theories
of relapse (Witkiewitz & Marlatt, 2004) such as family history, self-efficacy, and social
support. Second, just as the relapse process is dynamic, the factors promoting or protecting
against relapse can also be dynamic (Van Zundert et al., 2010; Witkiewitz & Marlatt, 2004).
Future developments will incorporate the proximal, time-varying risk and protective factors.

Third, our sample contained only ~25% individuals of minoritized racial or ethnic identities,
and only collected information on sex assigned at birth but not gender identity. Fourth, the
limitations – as well as the benefits – of the retrospective TLFB (relative to prospective
daily methods) should be considered. Indeed, when examining longer timeframes, the TLFB
tends to give weekly patterns rather than precise daily use (Barringer et al., 2022; Dulin
et al., 2017), although it is reasonably precise with shorter time series (Hoeppner et al.,
2010). With that said, the retrospective TLFB is still the better method to use in a criminal
justice-involved population of polydrug users then prospective daily monitoring; indeed,
many of our participants did not have a stable housing situation, or a flip phone; some
were reincarcerated at the time of follow-ups. Most importantly, data in prospective daily
assessments are generally missing systematically, i.e., on days participants are planning
to use, using, or experiencing high levels of negative affect. Moreover, compliance with
daily prospective protocols in criminal justice-involved samples tends to be quite low, and
overtaxing participants with daily assessments can lead to study dropout (Tonkin et al.,
2023). In the future, a combination protocol might be preferred in such samples (e.g.,
‘bursts’ of daily assessment, and weekly, biweekly, or monthly retrospective assessments via
the TLFB).

Author Manuscript

Fifth, we evaluated the predictive utility of the parameters by testing their associations
with a range of clinically relevant outcomes such as criminal behaviors, and social and
occupational functioning. Ideally, we would have included the long-term patterns of use
over two to three years. Sixth, the model in its current iteration can only be estimated in
participants with variance in use patterns rather than participants showing exclusively a
single well of use or non-use. As with virtually all statistical models, the DWPM is unable
to handle constant behavior and it is better suited to sporadic use rather than exclusive
abstinence. A treatment sample dominated by the non-use well is interesting (and can
suggest the treatment worked); however, it is not clear how the dynamics will replicate in a
different, non-treatment-seeking sample. Likewise, the nomothetic associations between the
dynamic parameters and outcome variables may be different in other samples.

Author Manuscript

Seventh, we assumed the parameters of the DWPM to be constant within person over
time. Yet, the underlying dynamic processes immediately following treatment may be very
different from processes that happen later when routines, setbacks, and stressors occur.
Future work will incorporate the time-varying nature of these parameters. Future studies
should also use data collected on different timescales as this can have nontrivial effect
on the resulting dynamics (Boker et al., 2009). Another future direction is to examine
the dynamics separately by type of substance, as different substances may have different
patterns depending on their unique properties such as the intensity and duration of effect.
Finally, collecting data on dose and timing (e.g., amount or length of within-day use) would
allow us to differentiate controlled use from problem use.
In sum, the present study introduces a viable tool to model substance use relapse as a
dynamic process. On a broader theoretical level, the DWPM is consistent with modern
conceptualizations of relapse: relapse is a dynamic (rather than static) process where
changing between states of use and non-use is expected (Witkiewitz & Marlatt, 2004). In
contrast with the nomothetic, arbitrary definitions of relapse, the DWPM offers an empirical,

personalized definition of relapse based on their current latent state and its history. The
DWPM describes the relapse process using the analogy of separation energy, or the amount
of disturbance required to move the system from abstinence to relapse and vice versa. Based
on this separation energy, we can determine individualized risk of relapse. This feature is
absent in other dynamical systems models applied to substance use such as cusp catastrophe
and regime switching models. Moreover, the DWPM has the additional benefit of flexibility,
such that this model can be fit to a single individual, a group (with fixed effects), or as a
random effects model; this flexibility is patently absent in other dynamical systems models.
Additionally, the DWPM can assist in efforts to distinguish a single use event (lapse) from
relapse based on the person-specific dynamic process (Maisto et al., 2016). Traditionally,
lapse is defined as the initial use after an attempt to quit while relapse is returning to
previous patterns of use. We, by contrast, regard this as the difference between an observed
state of use and a latent state of use. That is, single or occasional use is regarded as
measurement error, or perhaps a dynamic innovation that fails to move the latent state out
of the abstinence well and into the relapse well; whereas relapse is when the latent state is
in the use well. Finally, the model and associated personalized definitions of relapse use all
available data in the time series, rather than just a few datapoints of use to determine relapse.
Thus, the present study represents a step forward in understanding relapse as a latent process
and moves the field towards personalized addiction medicine.
