                          Twelfth Anniversary.
   The Twelfth Anniversary was held in the Broadway Tabernacle, on
the 11th of May     the President, Chancellor Walworth, in the chair.
                      ;



Prayer was offered by the Rev. Dr. Hutton, of New York. The
President read an address in which he particularly directed the friends
of Temperance to the right education of children, and to securing on
behalf of the cause, religious influence.        The annual Report was
read by the Rev. John Marsh, Cor. Sec, and able addresses were
made by John P Hale, Esq. United States Senator, from New Hamp-
shire Dr. J. Vionis, in French, from France, interpreted by Rev. E.
      ;



N. Kirk, of Boston Rev. Dr. Tyng J. H. W. Hawkins, and Rev.
                          ;                  ;


Mr. King, of Dublin.          A
                              collection was taken up of $137.70, for the
spread of the cause.
   The Report expressed gratitude for what had been accomplished,
but undiminished indignation and distress at continued evils. -The
desolating traffic is still in the land, licensed by law and upheld by
 public sentiment.    Twenty-three million gallons of pure alcohol in
 various mixtures, here annually pass from the vender to the consum-
 er to make drunkards, distress families, fill up poor-houses, and de-
 grade the nation. In Great Britain are consumed 28 million gal-
 lons of this deceitful, destructive poison; in Prussia, 15,900,000
 gallons; in Sweden, 11 million; in France, 137 millions.       In these
 five countries are annually expended, according to official returns,
 546 million dollars for intoxicating drinks. In Great Britain and the

United States 100,000 men perish annually from drunkenness.            But
this is a day of political and moral revolution, and a day of hope.     In
the past year, the friends of the cause have been discomfited by an
action of legislation, antagonistical to the will of the people, ex-
pressed at the ballot box.      Grants have been retracted. In New
York State, after a majority of 68,000 votes given against the licensed
traffic, the State is again burdened with it.    Vermont, after giving a
majority of 8,000 against the licensed traffic, hangs in an evil balance.
New Hampshire has instructed her legislature by a vote of 2 to 1, to
make a prohibitory statute. Massachusetts is hesitating what to do.
Maine is firm against the traffic. The Western States are boldly
contesting the right of the rum-seller to flood them with pauperism
and crime. The Report alluded to several causes of discouragement,

                                 Tlae False God.
   Among the many peculiar customs of the Chinese, there is one
peculiarly peculiar.  Among the many false gods which they wor-
ship,if, perchance, there be one to whom for years they have offered
the most costly sacrifices, and poured out the richest oblations, to
whom they have knelt and prayed, and for all these have received
nothing in return, they charge it with being a false god.      " For all
our offerings we have received nothing
                         .                     ;this is^ no true God.
There is no profit in his worship, " they say. The accusation is en-
tered, and they have a trial.   The mandarin sits in judgment. Pro-
clamation is made that any shall show cause, if he can, why the god
should be dethroned.     The evidence is received, and then, if the god
be condemned, sentence is passed, its altars are cast down, and its
worship made infamous.      My friends, is there not a false god in this
country too ?    Have we not been worshipping a false god, presenting
costly incense, and offering up our most precious possessions ?    And
have we not been doing this for years ? What profit has there been
in his worship ?    Why should we not have a trial of this false god,
and have it to-day? I should like to attend such a trial, before the
whole American people. His priests should be called upon for his
defence, and his worshippers should testify, and show cause, if they
can, why he is not a false god.   I would call up Old Age, with his
tottering footsteps.   And I would ask, Show me what profit there is
in this worship ?"   With trembling voice he would answer, " My first-
born and my last-born has he taken from me and in youth they
                                                       ;



went down to the grave of sons and daughters has he robbed me
                             ;                                         ;

yes, and health, and wealth, and peace of mind        the wife of my
                                                           ;


youth, and the stay of my declining years, all, all have been sacri-
ficed, and naught is left but an infamous name and a drunkard's
grave.''  What profit is this ? And I would call up woman to give
her testimony, with her crushed affections, and blighted hopes, and
weeping eyes. And what return has she received but poverty and
disgrace, and an early grave ?   So we would go through every de-
partment of society, and every walk of life not only through the
                                                   ;



hut of poverty, but the parlor of wealth, the hall of science, and the
temple of religion. Yea, I would summon the Church, and she
would say that her most promising and gifted sons, her brightest and
best ornaments, at the very time when they were sounding the alarm
on the watch-towers of Zion, when preaching of " temperance, right-
eousness, and a judgment to come," have fallen.      The destroyer
crept into the sanctuary, and laid his hand upon the preacher and
laid him low.   And so he has entered the hall of justice, and the
Senate, and every place where there were victims to be sacrificed.
Why, then, when the testimony is so conclusive, when the judgment
and the reason are convinced, why is not the verdict rendered and

                         Affecting Scene,
  A gentleman writing to the Star of Temperance relates the fol-
lowing affecting tale. Early, upon one of the bright mornings with
which heaven hath blessed us this winter, a little girl, of eleven years
of age, was seen hurrying from a grog-shop, to her home with a
bottle of whisky.     In a short time she was again seen travelling the
same course, and again, and again, and again, did she carry to her
home the destroying cup, till another morning dawned and death
had taken from this beautiful and interesting girl her mother. An
inquest was held over the body, and the little girl with her eyes full
of tears, and her soul pouring out its grief, gush after gush, stated
before the coroner's jury, that she had carried ten pints of whisky
the former day and that night, to her parents, who drank nearly the
whole, and from the effects of which, the jury decided her mother
died.   When the dead body of the little girl's mother was laid out
for burial by the public officer, her father was suffering with delirium
tremens.    And, the sad remnant of her whom he once promised to
love and cherish in sickness and in health, the mother of his child, he
cursed and swore whilst his brain was crazed by the influence of the
destroyer, so as to make the hardest heart present shrink back with
astonishment and horror and in this state, I am informed, he too on
                         ;



the next day paid the last demand of nature, leaving his child in the
worst part of our city without a friend and, unless the protection of
                                        ;



some one, moved by a just sense of duty shall be yielded to her, to
become an early prey to the licentious and the cruelly wicked.      Om
people are not easily moved by such transactions, for they are too
common.      The public mind has become so accustomed to such
scenes that it is not disturbed by their .occurrence. It submits to the
worst wrongs and grossest outrages upon humanity with as much
apparent unconcern as the poor slave, whose back has been scarred
and lacerated times without number, does to his condition. God
speed the day, when like the trump of an awakening angel, the con-
dition of our people shall arouse them to action.

                        BY PROFESSOR ALDEN.
  As John and Henry were going home from school, they saw, at a
a distance, a number of boys following a man, who was moving
slowly along the road.    Once in a while, he would turn about, and
take a few steps towards them, when they would turn and run away
from him.
   " What is going on yonder?" said Henry.
   " It is a parcel of boys, making fun of a drunken man, I guess,"
said John.
   " Let's cut across the fields, and not meet them," said Henry.
   " I will not turn out of my way, for a drunken man," said John.
24                  AMERICAN TEMPERANCE ALMANAC.

   John had better taken Henry's advice, for when the drunken man
came near them, he proved to be John's own father. There he was
staggering along, his limbs trembled as if he had the palsy, and he
was talking to himself and swearing, while the wicked boys were
throwing sticks and mud at him, and provoking him to swear more
and more. He did not know his son, as he passed him, and the boys
did not know that he was John's father.       Bad as they were, they
had feeling enough to stop abusing the drunkard, when Henry told
them he was John's father.
   " Let us go on home," said John to Henry, " I can't do anything
for him."    Henry felt sorry for his companion, but as he could not
think of anything to say, in the way of comforting him, he was silent.
   The reason why the boys did not know that the drunken man was
John's father, was this he lived in a remote part of the township
                         :
                                                                            ;


John did not live at home. His mother was dead, and he had no
sisters.  I do not know how it came to pass, but the fact was, that
he had a good home in a religious family.
   " I shall never be a drunkard," said John, after he and Henry had
walked homewards, for some time in silence.
   " I hope not," said Henry.    Certainly we should not expect that
John would be a drunkard. He had had bitter experience of the
horrid effects of drunkenness.    He had seen his mother carried to
the grave by it ; for she died of a broken heart, occasioned by the
cruel conduct of her husband.     He had often felt the same mortifi-
cation which he must have felt when he saw those boys abusing his
father.   He knew the blessed effects of temperance he had ex-
                                                             ;


perience of it in his new home, where he was treated as a son.
Surely he was the last one that we should expect would be a drunk-
ard.    He was just the one to say, " I shall never be a drunkard."
   Where is John now ? His body is mouldering in a drunkard's
grave.   He did become a drunkard.
   Henry is still living. When the temperance pledge came about,
Henry signed it at once. John declined doing so he had seen

enough, he said, to keep him from being a drunkard, without signing

and died a drunkard's death. It is not enough for a man to say, " I
shall never be a drunkard."   He must say, " / will never begin to
drink!''



            What is the Harm in Sweet Cider 1
  We readily admit that sweet cider, just pressed from the apple, is
harmless.   It will not intoxicate, but where is the dividing line ?   At
how many days old may we safely drink it ?     This is hard to define,
for sometimes from the peculiar state of the atmosphere, it will in-
toxicate very early, and again it may remain for nearly a week free
from any intoxicating quality. The cider passes so insensibly from
                                d                                 :   !   -       !       ;

one state to the other, we are liable to be deceived at any moment
when we tamper with it. If an individual has a quantity of cider on
hand, as our farmers generally do, when they make it and drink of
it as soon as pressed, he will continue to drink it until it has become

impregnated with the intoxicating element, and first thing he knows,
he is drunk on cider. It is evident then, that the use of sweet cider
is one of the many by-ways to intemperance.          Avoid these ways,
then, and you will never become a drunkard.      It is quite remarkable
that sweet cider is such an obstacle in the way of some.      Every man
lias to do without sweet cider ten months in the year, but few are
willing to do without it the other two.     If any man loves the cause
of temperance, and desire's to promote its prosperity, he certainly
ought to be willing to sacrifice the luxury of drinking sweet cider two
months in the year, when no vital principle is involved. He must be
a poor temperance man that cannot make such a sacrifice.

             Mrs. Sigoiirney ©it Health and Dress.
   Physical as well as intellectual welfare should be duly consulted.
Regularity in the hours of rising and retiring, perseverance in exer-
cise and adaptation of costume to the variations of climate, and tem-
perance in all things are necessary branches of a sanitary regimen.
Exercise in the open air should be daily taken by the young.       But
since it is sometimes impossible to do so, young ladies should ac-
quaint themselves with some forms of domestic industry which will
be a substitute for out of door exercise. Riding on horseback and sea
bathing are powerful tonics for a delicate tissue of nerves. Without
health both industry and enjoyment languish therefore observe well
                                                             ;


 physical laws.
   In dress, avoid such a style as opposes taste, produces deformity
or leads to unnecessary expense.       Never permit fashion to impair
health, for if you do, so the " evil days " of disease will come, " and
 the years draw nigh," when as far as health is concerned, it must be
 said " there is no pleasure in them."   Stricture in the region of the
 lungs and heart is deeply perilous.    Some knowledge of anatomy is
 a requisite part of female education, that we may know partially how
 that " harp of a thousand strings, is made and kept in tune so long."
 Dress should never infringe on delicacy, nor engross too much time.
 Simplicity and grace are the elements of its power to charm.
